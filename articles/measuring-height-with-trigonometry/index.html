How tall is that tree? Measuring heights with trigonometry

You cannot climb a tree with a measuring tape, but you can measure an angle and a distance on the ground. That is enough.

By Ranjeet Choudhary Sir · 23 November 2026

Surveyors find the heights of buildings, hills and towers without ever climbing them. The trick is a right triangle and one trigonometric ratio.

The idea

Stand at a known distance d from the foot of the tree. Look up at the top and measure the angle of elevation θ (a simple protractor with a straw and a thread works). The tree, the ground and your line of sight form a right triangle, so

tan θ = height ÷ d, which gives height = d × tan θ.

An example

You stand 20 m from a tree and the angle of elevation of its top is 45°. Since tan 45° = 1, the height is 20 × 1 = 20 m. If the angle were 60°, the height would be 20 × √3 ≈ 34.6 m.

If your eyes are 1.5 m above the ground, add 1.5 m, because the angle is measured from your eye level, not from the ground.

When you cannot reach the foot

For a hill or a tower across a river, you cannot measure d directly. Take two readings instead: the angle from one point, then walk a known distance straight towards it and measure the angle again. Two angles and one distance give two equations, and you can solve for both the height and the distance. This is exactly the kind of 5-mark question you see in Class 10 boards.

Try it: Measure the angle of elevation of the top of your school building from 30 m away. If it is 30°, the building is 30 × (1/√3) = 10√3 ≈ 17.3 m tall (plus your eye height).
