Probability · Class 12

23 people, 50% chance: the birthday surprise

In a class of just 23 students, there is a better than even chance that two share a birthday. Probability explains why our intuition is wrong.

By Ranjeet Choudhary Sir · 23 November 2026

How many people do you need in a room before it is more likely than not that two of them share a birthday? Most people guess around 180, half of 365. The real answer is 23.

Count the opposite

It is hard to count all the ways two or more people can share a birthday. It is much easier to find the probability that nobody does, and subtract from 1. (Ignore 29 February and assume every day is equally likely.)

  • The first person can have any birthday: 365/365.
  • The second must avoid that day: 364/365.
  • The third must avoid two days: 363/365, and so on.

For n people, P(all different) = (365 × 364 × … × (365 − n + 1)) / 365ⁿ.

The numbers

PeopleP(at least one shared birthday)
10about 12%
23about 51%
30about 71%
50about 97%

Why it surprises us

We think about one person matching our own birthday. But the question is about any pair. With 23 people there are ²³C₂ = 253 different pairs, and each pair is a fresh chance for a match.

Connect it to your syllabus

This uses the multiplication rule and the idea of a complementary event, P(A) = 1 − P(not A), from Class 10 and Class 12 Probability, plus combinations from Class 11.

Try it: Check the birthdays in your own class. If you have 30 or more students, a shared birthday is more likely than not.
